ZATCA e-invoicing is usually treated as a finance project. In a hotel group, the data that makes an invoice compliant is created long before the invoice reaches accounts payable — in the requisition, the purchase order, and the goods receipt.

That is why integration projects stall. The finance team buys an e-invoicing solution, connects it to the accounting system, and then discovers that the procurement side cannot reliably answer basic questions: Which purchase order does this invoice belong to? Was the item received? Is the supplier’s VAT record current? Which property is the buyer?

Phase 1 and Phase 2 are different problems

Phase 1 is about generating compliant invoice documents. Phase 2 is about integrating with ZATCA’s Fatoora platform: exchanging invoice data, receiving clearance or reporting responses, and handling the technical artifacts that accompany a compliant invoice.

The exact obligations depend on the invoice type and the wave your business falls into. As a working rule, standard tax invoices generally need clearance before they are shared with the buyer, while simplified invoices are reported within the window ZATCA specifies. Confirm the current requirements for your entity and activity with ZATCA or your tax adviser; do not treat a blog post as the specification.

Important: this article is an operational checklist, not tax or legal advice. ZATCA requirements change by wave and invoice type. Validate your implementation against the current official specification.

Where procurement data breaks compliance

Supplier records are incomplete or stale

An e-invoice needs accurate seller and buyer identifiers, addresses, and VAT details. In many groups, supplier data lives in three places: a finance system, a purchasing spreadsheet, and a buyer’s inbox. The e-invoicing integration then fails at the worst possible moment — when an invoice is due.

Treat the supplier master as a controlled record. Validate VAT numbers at onboarding, record the legal name and address, and define who can change those fields. Keep an audit trail of changes.

Purchase orders and invoices do not reconcile

Procurement creates a purchase order for ten items. The supplier delivers eight, substitutes one, and invoices for all ten. Finance receives a compliant XML document that is operationally wrong.

The fix is not in the e-invoicing middleware. It is in the handoff between purchasing, receiving, and accounts payable. A goods receipt note that records accepted, rejected, and outstanding quantities gives finance something to match against. Without it, compliance becomes a document-generation exercise disconnected from the underlying transaction.

Tax treatment is not captured at the item level

Hotels buy a wide mix of goods and services: food and beverage, utilities, maintenance, professional services, and guest amenities. The tax treatment may differ by item or supplier. If the item master only stores a description and a price, someone has to reconstruct the tax logic on every invoice.

Capture the tax category and any exemptions at the item or supplier level, with a review path for exceptions. Make the default explicit rather than implicit.

Credit notes and corrections are an afterthought

Returns, short deliveries, and price corrections are normal in hospitality. Each one may require a credit note or debit note with a reference to the original invoice. If the procurement system cannot link the correction to the original transaction, the finance team has to reconstruct the relationship manually.

An operational readiness checklist

Before the integration go-live, I would want clear answers to these questions:

  • Is every active supplier’s legal name, address, and VAT identifier validated and owned by a named team?
  • Can every invoice be traced to a purchase order and a goods receipt, including partial deliveries?
  • Are item tax categories and exemptions stored, reviewable, and versioned?
  • Are credit notes and debit notes linked to the original invoice?
  • Is there a single invoice sequence per entity, with no gaps caused by retries or manual overrides?
  • Are clearance and reporting responses stored alongside the invoice, not just the final PDF?
  • Is there an exception queue for rejected, warning, or timed-out submissions?
  • Can the team replay a failed submission without creating a duplicate invoice?
  • Are roles separated between preparer, approver, and submitter?
  • Is there a tested archive for XML, QR artifacts, and acknowledgement responses?

Integration patterns that hold up

Keep one system of record for the commercial transaction and one integration boundary for ZATCA. In most hotel groups, the accounting or ERP system remains the invoice system of record; procurement supplies the purchase order, receipt, and supplier data.

The integration should be idempotent. A timeout does not mean the invoice was rejected, and a retry should not create a second invoice. Store the request identifier, the response, and the final status. Make the failure modes visible to an operator who can act on them.

Test with realistic hotel data: multi-property buyers, mixed tax categories, partial deliveries, supplier corrections, and Arabic names. A demo invoice with one line item will not expose the problems your team will face in the first week.

Compliance is a data discipline

The organizations that struggle with ZATCA are rarely struggling with XML. They are struggling with the quality of the commercial data behind the XML. Fix the supplier master, the item master, and the receiving process, and the compliance layer becomes much less dramatic.

Treat e-invoicing as the last mile of a procurement process, not a separate universe. That is the difference between a project that goes live and a process that stays live.
